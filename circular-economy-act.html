The Circular Economy Act, Explained

The EU has set recycled-content targets it cannot yet meet. This is the law meant to fix that — by building a single market for recycled material itself.

The missing piece

Recent EU packaging law tells companies what recycled content they must use. It does not fix the reason the material is hard to get: recycled material barely moves across borders. Recycling standards, recycled-content rules and waste classifications differ from one member state to the next, so what counts as a usable secondary raw material in one country may still count as waste in another.

The Circular Economy Act is the framework law meant to close that gap. Positioned under the EU's Clean Industrial Deal and Competitiveness Compass, its central aim is to create a genuine single market for secondary raw materials — recycled content that can be traded across the EU as a reliable, standardised commodity rather than a patchwork of national definitions.

The number that frames it

The EU measures progress with the circular material use rate: the share of material used in the economy that comes from recycling rather than newly extracted resources. The target is to roughly double it.

Almost nine of every ten material inputs into the EU economy are still newly extracted. The Act aims to double the recycled share within roughly four years — from a rate that has been stagnating, not rising. See the current figure, live from Eurostat →

What the Act is expected to do

A legislative proposal is scheduled for the second half of 2026, so the detail is still forming. But the direction the Commission has set out is clear. Expect it to:

  • Create a single market for secondary raw materials — the headline aim, giving recycled content consistent quality standards and free movement across the EU.
  • Harmonise end-of-waste criteria — one EU-wide answer to when a recovered material stops being "waste" and becomes a product, replacing divergent national rules.
  • Streamline Extended Producer Responsibility — aligning the schemes that make producers pay for end-of-life handling, which today differ scheme by scheme and country by country.
  • Reform e-waste rules — modernising the framework for electrical and electronic waste to recover more critical raw materials.
  • Push circular public procurement and digital product passports — using public buying power and better product data to pull demand toward circular material.

Why it matters here

This is where the three pieces connect. The PPWR sets binding recycled-content targets. Chemical recycling and mass balance attribution are two ways to hit them. But all of that depends on recycled material actually existing, at scale, as something a company can buy with confidence across borders.

The Circular Economy Act is the attempt to build that supply side. If PPWR is the demand — you must use this much recycled content — the Circular Economy Act is meant to be the market that makes the supply possible. Whether it delivers is the open question of the next few years.

Worth watching, not yet decided

Because the proposal is still being drafted, the honest position is that the ambition is set but the mechanisms are not. The circularity rate has stagnated for years, and a framework law only works if the harmonised standards behind it are strong enough to make cross-border trade in recycled material genuinely work. That is the detail to watch as the text emerges.
