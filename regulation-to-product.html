What Sustainability Regulations Actually Require of Software Products

Regulations don't ship features. Product teams do — and the translation from one to the other is where most compliance products quietly get it wrong.

A regulation is not a spec

It's tempting to treat a regulation's text as something close to a requirements document — read it, extract the rules, build the fields. But a regulation is written for legal certainty, not for implementation. It's ambiguous where legislators couldn't agree, precise only where enforcement demanded it, and structured around obligations and dates, not around data models or user flows.

Three things a regulation gives a product team directly: the obligations (what must be true), the evidence requirements (what must be documented and provable), and the dates (when each obligation becomes enforceable). Three things it never gives you: the data model, the interface, or the prioritization. Those are product decisions the regulation is silent on — and silence gets filled by whoever builds first, well or badly.

A concrete case: PPWR's conformity requirement

From regulation to requirement

The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation requires that packaging "undergo a conformity assessment," backed by an EU declaration of conformity and technical documentation. That's one sentence of law. It is not a product requirement — it's a destination three or four requirements away.

Read literally as a feature, it becomes "add a document upload field." Read as an obligation a business has to sustain over years, it becomes something closer to: a versioned document store tied to a SKU, not a product; an audit trail proving which version of a declaration was live on which date; and a way to track that a conformity record itself has a shelf life — because packaging changes, suppliers change, and a declaration filed in 2026 doesn't stay true forever.

Same sentence of law. Very different systems, depending on whether the team modelled the obligation or just the document.

The trap: building for today's numbers

PPWR's recycled-content targets aren't a single number — they're 30% for beverage bottles, 30% for other contact-sensitive PET packaging, 10% for other contact-sensitive plastics, 35% for everything else, from 2030 — and they rise again by 2040. A team that hardcodes "30%" as a constant has built something that works today and breaks silently the moment the threshold changes or a new packaging category is added.

This is the pattern across most sustainability regulation: today's number is a snapshot of a value that moves on a known schedule, or via delegated acts that aren't written yet. Design-for-recycling criteria under PPWR, for instance, are still being adopted through delegated acts due in 2028 — the obligation exists in law now; the detailed rule doesn't yet. A product built only for what's currently in force has already started accumulating technical debt against a law that hasn't finished being written.

What this means for product requirements

  • Model obligations as versioned, dated rules — not hardcoded constants — so a threshold change is a data update, not a release.
  • Treat evidence and documentation as first-class objects with their own lifecycle, not attachments bolted onto an existing record.
  • Separate "what the law requires" from "what we can enforce today" — build for the version of the regulation that's coming, not just the one that's in force.
  • Assume amendment, not stability — delegated acts, phased dates, and national derogations are the norm in EU sustainability law, not the exception.

Why this matters beyond compliance software

I've done this translation from the regulatory side, not the product side — most recently assessing a 1,000+ SKU packaging portfolio against PPWR requirements, where the gap between "what the regulation says" and "what a system needs to track" was the actual work. The pattern above is what that gap looks like from the inside: regulations are stable in structure and unstable in detail, and the products built on top of them need to be designed for that shape, not just for this year's numbers.
