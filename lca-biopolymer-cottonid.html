Life Cycle Assessment of a Bio-Based Vulcanised Fibre (Cottonid)

A cradle-to-gate LCA of a cellulose-based engineering material — and a reminder that the lowest-carbon option is rarely the lowest-impact one.

Why this study

Most engineering plastics are made from fossil feedstock. Replacing them with bio-based alternatives is one of the more obvious decarbonisation levers available to manufacturers — and one of the least examined. A material described as bio-based is frequently assumed to be the better choice on that basis alone, without anyone having measured what it costs to produce.

This study was my M.Sc. thesis at the Technical University of Munich. It puts numbers to that assumption for one specific material, and then tests whether changing the feedstock improves the result. The answer is more interesting than a simple yes.

The material

Vulcanised fibre is one of the oldest engineered materials still in industrial use. It is made by treating layered sheets of paper with zinc chloride, which swells the cellulose and fuses the layers into a single dense sheet. The chemical is then washed out and the material dried. There is no resin and no fossil polymer — the finished product is essentially bonded cellulose.

The result is stiff, tough, machinable and electrically insulating, which is why vulcanised fibre has long been used for things like electrical insulation, gaskets, abrasive disc backings and protective components. In other words, it competes with engineering plastics while being made almost entirely from a renewable raw material.

The product assessed here is Cottonid, produced at commercial scale by Hornex in Germany. The assessment follows ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.

What was measured

A bio-based input does not by itself make a material better. The study quantified the production burden of the reference product, then modelled three alternative feedstocks to see whether the profile could be improved:

  • Cottonid-R (reference) — 70% textile-waste pulp, 30% virgin wood pulp
  • Cottonid-A — 70% textile-waste pulp, 30% recycled wood pulp
  • Cottonid-B — 100% textile-waste pulp
  • Cottonid-C — 100% recycled paper

The assessment stops at the factory gate. Everything after it — service life, reuse, disposal — sits outside the boundary, which is why these results cannot be used to compare Cottonid against another material over a whole life cycle.

Method

Scope and modelling
Functional unit
1 kg of Cottonid, from 1.2 m² of paper, 3 mm thick, 135 g/m², 18 layers.
Foreground data
From the manufacturer by questionnaire: 2.88 MJ electricity, 10.8 MJ natural gas, 0.05 kg of 70% zinc chloride solution and 1 kg mixed-pulp paper per kg of Cottonid. Full recycling of the zinc chloride solution is assumed.
Background data
Sphera and ecoinvent. Textile-waste pulping and mixed-pulp papermaking were built from published literature, as no dataset existed.
Software and method
GaBi. CML-2016 midpoint, nine impact categories.

Global warming potential is driven by the energy used in pulp and paper production, not by the vulcanising step. The two negative figures are not errors: substituting textile waste for virgin cellulose avoids fertiliser-related nutrient loading, which appears as a net credit in eutrophication and freshwater ecotoxicity.

Across the normalised profile, marine aquatic ecotoxicity dominates by a wide margin, followed by fossil resource depletion. Carbon is not the largest story in this material.
