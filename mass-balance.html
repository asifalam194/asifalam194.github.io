Mass Balance, Explained

Why a certified sustainable product is chemically identical to the conventional one — and why that is the point, not a loophole.

The most common misunderstanding

When someone buys a mass-balance certified product, they usually assume it physically contains bio-based or recycled material. It does not — not in any measurable way. The product is chemically identical to its fossil-based equivalent.

What differs is what entered the production system, and how that share is accounted for. That distinction sounds like a technicality. It is the entire concept.

The problem it solves

Chemical production does not happen on separate, dedicated lines. It happens in vast integrated networks where feedstocks enter at the very start and thousands of products emerge at the end. You cannot trace a single renewable molecule through that system, and building parallel plants for renewable feedstock alone would be prohibitively expensive.

So the real question becomes: how do you begin substituting fossil feedstock today, at scale, using the plants that already exist?

Mass balance is the answer. Alternative feedstock — bio-based or recycled — is fed into the existing network alongside fossil feedstock. The corresponding share is then attributed to specific products through a certified accounting method, audited by an independent third party.

Both outputs are chemically identical. Neither can be tested to reveal its origin.
Accounting layer — what is certified
300 kg alternative share attributed to Product A
Product B is sold as conventional. The share is counted once, and only once.

The two layers are deliberately drawn apart. The physical flow and the certified accounting are separate things — which is exactly why a mass-balance product contains no traceable "green molecule." Quantities are simplified; real attribution applies conversion factors to account for process losses.

The closest everyday analogy is green electricity. The electrons reaching your socket come from the same mixed grid as everyone else's. What you buy is a certified share of renewable generation fed into that grid — and that purchase is what drives more of it to be built.

Two branches

Biomass balance substitutes fossil feedstock with bio-based raw materials such as bio-naphtha or biomethane, derived from organic waste, crops or vegetable oils.

Chemical recycling substitutes it with feedstock recovered from plastic waste. Pyrolysis is the most common route: waste that would otherwise be incinerated or landfilled is thermally broken down into pyrolysis oil, which re-enters production as a raw material. This addresses mixed, contaminated and multi-layer plastics that mechanical recycling cannot handle.

Both use the same attribution logic. Only the feedstock differs.

Certification is what makes it credible

Without independent verification, mass balance would be an unauditable claim. Two schemes dominate in the European chemical industry: ISCC PLUS (International Sustainability and Carbon Certification) and REDcert². Both require third-party audits of feedstock inputs, production sites and product outputs, verifying that the alternative feedstock was genuinely purchased and fed into the system in the claimed quantity. The underlying chain-of-custody model is defined in ISO 22095.

Where the honest debate sits

Mass balance is a transition mechanism, not an end state. It is fairly criticised on two grounds: attribution rules differ between schemes and can be hard to compare, and describing a chemically identical product as "renewable" risks misleading buyers who assume physical content.

Both criticisms deserve to be taken seriously — the second is precisely why explanations like this one are needed. The strongest case for mass balance is not that it makes a product physically different. It is that it creates real, contractual demand for renewable and recycled feedstock in a system that cannot switch over all at once.
