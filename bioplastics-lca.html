Comparing Bioplastics: What Ten Years of Life Cycle Studies Actually Show

Published carbon figures for bio-based polymers disagree with each other by more than the polymers differ from each other. That is not a flaw in the data — it is the finding.

Start with a contradiction

NatureWorks has been unusually transparent about its Ingeo PLA plant, publishing four life cycle studies over more than a decade. The numbers did not move in the direction you would expect.

The 2006 production system was reported at 0.27 kg CO₂ eq per kg, an 85% reduction against the 2003 eco-profile. The 2015 study reported 0.62 — higher. The production data had not changed. The 2015 study used an updated background LCA database, and that alone accounts for the difference.

Same factory. Same polymer. Same company, publishing in good faith. The reported figure more than doubled because the background data underneath it was refreshed.

If a single well-documented plant can move that far without emitting anything differently, then a table ranking six bioplastics against each other from six different studies is not measuring the polymers. It is measuring the modelling choices.

How wide is the disagreement?

Wide enough to swallow every difference anyone is arguing about. Across published studies, reported global warming potential for producing one kilogram of PLA spans 0.1 to 3.1 kg CO₂-eq.

A thirtyfold spread for one polymer. The three causes identified are methodological inconsistency, feedstock source, and processing — not differences in the material itself. A wider review of bio-based plastic LCAs found variation exceeding 1000% for the same polymer across studies.

The single largest lever

Where you draw the system boundary. Studies that include end of life report substantially higher GWP than those stopping at resin or granule production, because the biogenic carbon taken up during feedstock growth is released again when the product is disposed of.

The PLA meta-analysis, drawing on more than 80 studies, quantifies exactly this.

Median values, kg CO₂ eq per kg. At the gate, PLA sits below PET, PP and PE. At the grave, it lands in the same territory as PET and PP. Both figures are correct. They answer different questions, and only one of them is the question a packaging decision actually asks.

This is the number that gets quoted selectively. A supplier citing 1.63 is not lying. They are answering a question about resin production while their customer is asking a question about a product.

Note the comparators disagree too. Polypropylene appears at 3.53 kg CO₂ eq/kg in one comparison. Polyethylene appears at 2.6 for HDPE and 2.9 for LDPE in another. The fossil baseline is not a fixed reference point either, which means the gap between a bioplastic and "conventional plastic" partly depends on which conventional plastic study you happen to pick up.

The other three levers

Biogenic carbon accounting Whether feedstock CO₂ uptake is credited at the gate, and whether its release is charged at end of life. Two competing conventions are in active use, and reviews consistently identify inconsistent treatment of carbon sequestration as a primary source of divergence.
Feedstock and land use Corn, sugarcane, orchard waste and waste glycerol give materially different answers for the same polymer, as the PHA figures above show. Whether indirect land-use change is inside the boundary varies between studies.
Inventory completeness Of 44 studies published between 2011 and 2020 comparing bioplastics against petrochemical plastics, only a small minority accounted for additives — despite additives being present in essentially every commercial plastic article.

Ten years of the evidence base

The interesting decade-long trend is not in the numbers. It is in what the reviewers concluded about the numbers.

2020

Walker and Rothman assess comparative LCAs of bio-based and fossil polymers against EU Product Environmental Footprint standards. No published article fully meets them. Of the 25 that partially comply, covering seven bio-based and seven fossil polymers across seven impact categories, it proves impossible to declare any polymer type lowest-impact in any category.

2021

Bishop and colleagues screen 44 studies published 2011–2020 and attribute the variation to specific methodological choices: system boundaries, land-use change treatment, biogenic carbon representation, allocation, and missing data for bio-based monomer conversion.

2024

Pinlova and colleagues pool more than 80 PLA studies and produce defensible medians rather than a single headline figure — the 1.63 and 3.91 above. This is the first point at which a PLA number can be quoted with a stated basis behind it.

2025

A fully transparent in-plant cradle-to-grave PHA assessment puts the polymer at 5.77 kg CO₂e/kg against 5.52 for conventional fossil plastics today, with improvement projected for 2030–35. The framing has shifted from advocacy to a dated, conditional claim.

Read together, that is a field maturing. A decade ago the literature largely asserted that bio-based polymers were better. It now says the comparison cannot yet be made reliably, and it says precisely why. That is progress, even though it reads like a retreat.

What this means in practice

If a supplier presents a carbon figure for a bio-based material, the number on its own carries almost no information. These are the questions that make it interpretable:

  • Where does the boundary stop? Gate or grave. If it is gate, the figure cannot support a claim about the product, only about the resin.
  • How is biogenic carbon treated? Credited at uptake, charged at release, or credited and never charged. The third is common and is where negative figures usually come from.
  • Which feedstock, and from where? The same polymer from corn and from waste glycerol are not the same product environmentally.
  • What end-of-life route is assumed, and does it exist? A composting assumption is only valid where industrial composting actually receives that material.
  • Which background database, and which version? As the opening example shows, this alone can move the figure by a factor of two.
  • Are additives included? Usually not.

None of this makes bio-based materials a bad choice. It makes an unqualified carbon comparison an unsafe basis for claiming one — which matters increasingly, as substantiation requirements tighten across EU product and packaging law.

Limitations of this review

  • This is a synthesis of published reviews and meta-analyses, not an independent systematic screening. Study counts behind individual polymer figures vary considerably and are not normalised here.
  • Figures are drawn from different studies with different functional units, geographies and electricity mixes. They are presented side by side to illustrate divergence, not to rank.
  • PLA is heavily over-represented in the literature relative to every other bio-based polymer, so the evidence base is much thinner for PHA, TPS and the drop-in polymers.
  • Only global warming potential is covered. As my LCA of a bio-based vulcanised fibre shows, the lowest-carbon option is frequently not the lowest-impact one once toxicity and eutrophication are included.
