PPWR, Explained

One change matters more than any individual target: packaging law stopped being a directive and became a regulation.

The shift that matters most

On 12 August 2026, the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (EU) 2025/40 replaced the EU's long-standing Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive. Where the directive gave member states flexibility in both regulation and implementation — meaning each country could take its own approach — the PPWR applies harmonised standards across the EU.

That sounds procedural. It is not. Under the directive, a company selling packaging into eight member states faced eight interpretations of the same law. Under the regulation, the rules apply directly and override conflicting national legislation. One rulebook, enforceable everywhere.

August 2026 was the starting gun, not the deadline

The application date created a widespread impression that everything is now in force. It is not. The regulation entered into force on 11 February 2025 with an 18-month lead-in, and is implemented in phases — different requirements become applicable over time.

What landed in August 2026:

  • Substances of concern. Packaging must be manufactured so their presence and concentration is minimised, with PFAS thresholds for food-contact packaging and limits on lead, cadmium, mercury and hexavalent chromium.
  • Conformity documentation. Packaging must undergo a conformity assessment, with an EU declaration of conformity supported by technical documentation.
  • EPR registration.

Everything with teeth arrives later.

The timeline

Feb 2025
Regulation enters into force. 18-month lead-in begins.

Applies. Directive repealed. Substances of concern and PFAS limits, conformity documentation, EPR registration.Now

Design-for-recycling criteria adopted through delegated acts. Harmonised labelling requirements become applicable.
2030
The hard deadline. All packaging must be recyclable. Minimum post-consumer recycled content in plastic packaging. 50% empty-space cap. Binding reuse targets. Six single-use formats banned.
2038
Grade C packaging excluded from the market.
2040
Recycled-content targets rise sharply — up to 65% for single-use plastic beverage bottles.

What actually lands in 2030

All packaging placed on the market must be recyclable, assessed through performance grades A, B and C. Packaging below a 70% recyclability rate may not be placed on the market from 2030; grade C is excluded from 2038.

On recycled content, Article 7 requires plastic packaging to contain a minimum share of post-consumer recycled plastic from 1 January 2030. The headline targets:

  • 30% — single-use plastic beverage bottles
  • 30% — other contact-sensitive packaging made primarily from PET
  • 10% — contact-sensitive packaging in other plastics
  • 35% — all other plastic packaging

Alongside these: a 50% cap on empty space in transport, grouped and e-commerce packaging, binding reuse targets, and six categories of single-use format banned outright.

The bottleneck no regulation can legislate away

Here is the part that gets least attention and will cause the most difficulty.

Only post-consumer recyclate counts toward the Article 7 targets. Post-industrial recyclate does not satisfy them, even though it is widely marketed as recycled content. And food-grade supply is constrained: mechanical recycling reliably produces food-grade material only for PET, while chemical recycling capacity remains limited.

So the targets are legally binding, and the material to meet them does not yet exist at the required volume. That gap is the real story of the next four years — and it is why chemical recycling and mass balance attribution have moved from sustainability projects to supply-chain necessities.

Where to start

The compliance work that matters now is unglamorous: know exactly what your packaging is made of, hold the documentation to prove it, and model your 2030 recycled-content position early enough to secure supply rather than scramble for it.
