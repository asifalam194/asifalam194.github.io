Life Cycle Assessment of a Bio-Based Vulcanised Fibre (Cottonid)

A cradle-to-gate LCA of a cellulose-based engineering material — and a clear case of the lowest-carbon option not being the lowest-impact one.

Why this study

Most engineering plastics are made from fossil feedstock. Replacing them with bio-based alternatives is one of the more obvious decarbonisation levers available to manufacturers — and one of the least examined. A material described as bio-based is frequently assumed to be the better choice on that basis alone, without anyone having measured what it costs to produce.

This study was my M.Sc. thesis at the Technical University of Munich. It puts numbers to that assumption for one specific material, then tests whether changing the raw paper feedstock improves the result. The answer turns out to depend entirely on which impact you look at.

The material

Vulcanised fibre is one of the oldest engineered materials still in industrial use. It is made by treating layered sheets of paper with zinc chloride, which swells the cellulose and fuses the layers into a single dense sheet. The chemical is then washed out and the material dried. There is no resin and no fossil polymer — the finished product is essentially bonded cellulose.

The result is stiff, tough, machinable and electrically insulating, which is why vulcanised fibre has long been used for electrical insulation, gaskets, abrasive disc backings and protective components. It competes with engineering plastics while being made almost entirely from a renewable raw material.

The product assessed here is Cottonid, produced at commercial scale by Hornex in Germany. The assessment follows ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.

What was measured

Cottonid-R is the reference product as actually manufactured, modelled on primary data from the factory. The other three are scenarios I constructed by varying the raw paper feedstock, to test whether a different input would give a better profile.

  • Cottonid-R — 70% textile-waste pulp, 30% sulphate pulp (reference, factory data)
  • Cottonid-A — 70% textile-waste pulp, 30% waste paper pulp
  • Cottonid-B — 100% textile-waste pulp
  • Cottonid-C — 100% waste paper pulp

The assessment stops at the factory gate. Everything after it — service life, reuse, disposal — sits outside the boundary, which is why these results cannot be used to compare Cottonid against another material over a whole life cycle.

Method

Scope and modelling
Functional unit
1 kg of Cottonid, from 1.2 m² of paper, 3 mm thick, 135 g/m², 18 layers.
Foreground data
From the manufacturer by questionnaire: 2.88 MJ electricity, 10.8 MJ natural gas, 0.05 kg of 70% zinc chloride solution and 1 kg mixed-pulp paper per kg of Cottonid. Full recycling of the zinc chloride solution is assumed.
Background data
Sphera and ecoinvent. Textile-waste pulping and mixed-pulp papermaking were built from published literature, as no dataset existed.
Software and method
GaBi. CML-2016 midpoint, nine impact categories.

The gap here is larger than for carbon. Waste paper pulp cuts fossil resource depletion by around 37%, while the other three sit within 2% of each other.

On the energy-driven indicators, then, the conclusion looks obvious. Cottonid-C is the best of the four on global warming, on fossil depletion and on acidification. A procurement decision made on carbon would stop here.

The inversion

It would be the wrong decision. Cottonid-C is the worst of the four scenarios in six of the nine categories — every toxicity indicator, plus eutrophication and photochemical ozone creation.

Against the reference product, marine aquatic ecotoxicity is roughly three times higher, human toxicity more than double, terrestrial ecotoxicity more than triple. Freshwater ecotoxicity flips from a net credit of −0.101 to a burden of 0.706, and eutrophication flips the same way. The three feedstocks containing textile waste all hold their negative eutrophication values; waste paper alone does not.

The rest of the picture is more evenly split than a single winner would suggest. The reference product is best on both marine and freshwater ecotoxicity. Cottonid-B, made entirely from textile waste, is best on human toxicity, terrestrial ecotoxicity, eutrophication and photochemical ozone creation. Cottonid-A is worst on carbon and fossil depletion and never best at anything, which makes it the one scenario that can be ruled out cleanly.

What it means

There is no single best feedstock here. There is a choice about which burden to carry: waste paper buys lower energy-related impacts and pays for it in toxicity, while textile waste does the reverse.

That trade-off is invisible to a carbon footprint. Ranking these four options on GWP alone would have selected the scenario with roughly three times the marine ecotoxicity of the product already being made — and the decision would have looked well-evidenced. This is the practical argument for full life cycle assessment over single-indicator footprinting, and it generalises well beyond one material: bio-based and recycled inputs shift impacts between categories at least as often as they reduce them.

Limitations

Stating these matters more than the results themselves. An LCA without honest boundaries is a marketing document.

  • No primary data were available for pulp or paper production. These stages rely on literature and generic database inventories — and they are the largest contributors to most impact categories.
  • No established dataset exists for pulp derived from textile waste, so the process was constructed from published inventory data.
  • Zinc chloride solution is absent from the databases used and was modelled stoichiometrically from zinc and hydrochloric acid inventories.
  • Waste outflows at each production stage were assumed negligible and are not modelled.
  • The assessment stops at the factory gate. Use-phase performance, service life and end of life are not covered, so these results do not support whole-life comparisons against other materials.
  • No sensitivity or uncertainty analysis was performed.
  • Results are specific to one manufacturer, one site and the German electricity grid mix, and are not generalisable to vulcanised fibre production elsewhere.
M.Sc. thesis, Technical University of Munich. Conducted under ISO 14040/44 using GaBi, with Sphera and ecoinvent background data. Product data provided by Hornex.
